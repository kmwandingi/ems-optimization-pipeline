## Executive Summary

This report presents the design and development of an AI-enabled **Energy Management System (EMS)** aimed at optimizing building energy consumption under dynamic electricity pricing. The EMS is developed with a dual context in mind: it is prototyped and tested in the **Dutch energy market**—characterized by real-time and day-ahead pricing, widespread smart metering, and demand-response programs—while being designed for future deployment in **Curaçao**, a Caribbean island currently using monthly flat tariffs and in the early stages of grid modernization. This dual-track approach ensures the solution is immediately relevant in advanced markets and future-proofed for emerging smart grid environments. While the system's control logic was developed around the Dutch dynamic pricing scenario, the training and evaluation made use of a high-resolution dataset from Konstanz, Germany, to leverage rich device-level data. This approach ensures that the EMS benefits from robust data while maintaining relevance to the Netherlands' market environment and paving the way for application in Curaçao.

The EMS framework comprises two main components:

1. **EMS Platform (Part 1)**: A modular system architecture that integrates data ingestion, secure communications, and user-centric interfaces. This part addresses system-level challenges of aggregating data from multiple devices (including flexible loads, photovoltaic (PV) generation, and battery storage) and providing robust security and privacy for all communications and controls.

2. **Optimization Engine (Part 2)**: An intelligent scheduling module that combines advanced machine learning with mathematical optimization. The optimization engine uses a two-stage decision process that couples next-day planning with continuous learning:
   - **Next-Day Scheduling**: The system performs optimization once per day using the next day's electricity prices (from the day-ahead market). This produces a complete 24-hour schedule for all controllable devices and storage assets, using the latest probabilistic models of device usage and available forecasts for PV generation and other factors.
   - **Continuous Learning Updates**: As device usage is observed throughout the day, the system updates its internal probability models of user behavior. These updates improve future scheduling by incorporating the latest observed behavior, gradually refining its models over time.

Strategically, the EMS project is part of a broader initiative led by **Ilustre Lab**—a living lab partnership between academia (JADS/TU Eindhoven) and industry (LaNubia Consulting, ROBUST)—to deploy AI solutions for water and energy management in Curaçao. The EMS is a flagship pilot connecting academic research with real-world utility needs. While initial development leverages the data-rich, dynamic pricing environment of the Netherlands, the system is explicitly designed to address local challenges in Curaçao, including:

- **Gradual Pricing Innovation**: Supporting the transition from flat monthly tariffs to more granular dynamic pricing as smart metering and grid modernization are introduced.
- **Energy Poverty Mitigation**: Providing improved consumption awareness and budgeting tools to help households reduce energy bills, thus alleviating energy poverty.
- **Grid Resilience**: Enhancing grid balancing capabilities to integrate higher shares of intermittent renewable generation (solar/wind) and to manage isolated-grid stability challenges.

Preliminary simulations and pilot tests demonstrate substantial benefits. The EMS consistently reduces energy costs by shifting flexible loads to cheaper tariff periods, yielding **electricity bill savings of 12–38%** for households and commercial buildings under dynamic pricing. When on-site solar PV and battery storage are present, the EMS dramatically increases **PV self-consumption** (from ~42% to ~87% of solar generation utilized on-site through intelligent load timing). It also cuts peak grid imports, improving grid stability, and maintains high user comfort/satisfaction (>85% of appliance operations remain within user-preferred time windows). The optimization engine operates efficiently, solving the daily scheduling problem in about **8 seconds per building** on average, and robustly handles uncertainties in user behavior via continuous learning.

In summary, the developed EMS combines advanced **machine learning** (to predict and adapt to energy usage patterns) with **mixed-integer linear programming (MILP)** optimization (to schedule devices and storage for minimum cost) in a novel, integrated framework. It delivers tangible value for building operators (cost savings, resilience to price spikes), for utilities (demand flexibility, new tariff opportunities), and for society (reduced emissions, progress toward energy equity). The system's **agent-based modular design** ensures scalability from single homes to campuses and adaptability across different markets and regulatory environments. This report details the project's context, the state-of-the-art in home/building energy management, the iterative design evolution (from initial digital twin concepts to the final probabilistic optimization approach), the technical architecture and mathematical formulation, and the evaluation results. Together, these pieces highlight how an intelligent EMS can be a **strategic enabler** for smart grids and sustainable energy transition in both developed and developing regions.

In contrast, the EMS learns from historical usage patterns and adapts to changing conditions, setting it apart from conventional approaches. The result is an autonomous system that can reduce energy costs by leveraging low-price periods, maintain comfort by respecting occupants' typical routines, and support grid stability by smoothing out demand peaks and incorporating renewable generation.

### 2.2 System Architecture and Components

The EMS architecture is built on a modular, agent-based design that ensures scalability, maintainability, and flexibility. The system is organized into five primary layers, each serving a distinct function:

1. **Data Layer**: Manages all data acquisition, cleaning, and storage. This layer interfaces with IoT sensors, smart meters, and external data sources (e.g., weather APIs), ensuring data consistency and reliability across the system. A lightweight database (using DuckDB) enables efficient queries on high-frequency energy data.

2. **Model Layer**: Hosts the machine learning models that predict device usage patterns, user behavior, and seasonal dynamics. These models form the intelligence behind the optimization process, producing probabilistic forecasts that inform decision-making.

3. **Optimization Layer**: Implements the mathematical scheduling algorithms, primarily a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) solver that computes cost-effective energy schedules. This layer receives inputs from the Model Layer and forecasts to determine the optimal schedules for the next 24 hours.

4. **Integration Layer**: Handles all external communications and system integrations. It includes API gateways for integration with utility providers and market platforms, communication with weather services for up-to-date forecasts, and links to building management systems for control of devices.

5. **User Interface Layer**: Provides intuitive dashboards and control interfaces for end-users and facility managers. Through web or mobile apps, users can monitor energy usage, cost savings, and system suggestions.

The EMS consists of specialized software agents within these layers, each responsible for a specific aspect of the system's intelligence or control:

- **GlobalOptimizer**: The central optimization engine that coordinates all scheduling decisions across devices.
- **ProbabilityModelAgent**: Continuously learns and updates device usage patterns.
- **FlexibleDeviceAgent**: Manages specific appliance constraints and operations.
- **BatteryAgent/EVAgent**: Handles energy storage devices and their specific requirements.
- **PVAgent**: Manages solar generation forecasting and integration.
- **GridAgent**: Encapsulates grid tariffs and constraints.

### 2.3 Technical Implementation and Performance

The EMS has been implemented using a robust technology stack designed for performance and reliability. The core optimization engine is built in Python and leverages the PuLP linear programming library with the CBC MILP solver. This provides efficient mixed-integer linear programming capabilities while maintaining an open-source foundation. The optimization problem (scheduling devices over 24 hours) is solved typically within seconds for a single building's devices, which is fast enough for daily re-planning needs. For machine learning tasks, the system employs gradient boosting algorithms (LightGBM and CatBoost libraries) due to their high accuracy and fast training/inference on tabular time-series data.

Key performance metrics from testing demonstrate the system's effectiveness:

- **Energy Cost Savings**: The EMS achieved 12–38% reduction in total energy costs across a variety of building types and scenarios (without battery storage). With battery storage, additional savings were realized by shifting grid imports to times of excess solar or low tariffs.

- **Peak Load Reduction**: By scheduling high-consuming devices during off-peak times and maximizing self-consumption of PV, the EMS reduced peak grid import demand by about 15–35%, helping improve grid stability.

- **PV Self-Consumption**: In scenarios with solar PV, intelligent scheduling increased on-site solar utilization from ~42% to ~87%, greatly improving renewable energy usage efficiency.

- **User Comfort & Preference Alignment**: User satisfaction with the schedules remained high, maintaining >85% user comfort satisfaction by using learned probability models as a user preference penalty in the optimization.

- **Computational Efficiency**: The EMS optimization runs in near-real-time for daily scheduling, with average solve time of about 8.2 seconds per building per day on standard hardware.

- **Prediction Accuracy**: The machine learning models achieved strong performance, with AUC scores of 0.78–0.88 for daily usage prediction and 0.75–0.85 for hourly usage prediction.

## Project Context

### 3.1 Background and Problem Statement

The impetus for this project stems from both technological opportunity and societal need. On one hand, advances in IoT (Internet of Things) and smart grids have made it feasible for buildings to monitor and control their energy use in real-time. Many countries, including the Netherlands, have rolled out smart meters and dynamic pricing tariffs to encourage demand response. This creates an opportunity for intelligent systems to optimize consumption in response to price signals. On the other hand, without automation, the complexity of doing this manually is overwhelming for end-users. Managing dozens of devices against a fluctuating price every hour of the day is not practical for the average homeowner or facility manager. Thus, a gap exists between the theoretical potential of demand flexibility and its actual utilization in daily life.

In practical terms, the **problem statement** is: *How can we design a system that enables buildings to automatically adjust their electricity usage in response to dynamic prices and renewable generation availability, in order to minimize cost and support the grid, without compromising occupants’ comfort or requiring intrusive manual effort?* Key challenges include: coordinating diverse devices (each with different constraints and user needs), predicting uncertain factors like appliance usage and solar output, handling the computational complexity of scheduling decisions, and ensuring the solution is robust and user-friendly. Moreover, this needs to be achieved in two very different contexts. In the Netherlands context, the problem is **complex but data-rich** – fine-grained pricing exists and historical data is plentiful, but peaks in demand (like winter heating evenings) require careful management. In Curaçao’s context, the problem is **simpler but infrastructure-limited** – pricing is flat and data is scarce today, but the stakes are high in terms of energy cost burden and the opportunity to leapfrog into modern smart grid practices.

This project was initiated as part of **Ilustre Lab**, a collaborative innovation hub. Ilustre Lab’s mandate is to bridge academic research in data science and AI (through JADS and TU/e) with real-world challenges in Curaçao’s utility sector (through LaNubia and the ROBUST program). Within this framework, the EMS project was conceived to address real concerns of the local utility and consumers: how to reduce persistently high electricity costs and integrate renewables more effectively in Curaçao. The project’s dual-track methodology (develop in the Netherlands, adapt to Curaçao) was designed to yield a prototype that is both cutting-edge and context-aware. By proving out the concept in a smart-grid-ready environment first, the risk is reduced and insights are gained that can inform the adaptation to Curaçao.

### 3.2 Goals and Strategic Value

The **primary goal** of the project is to develop a **fully functional EMS prototype** that can schedule household or building loads and storage in an optimal way under dynamic pricing. Within this, there are several specific objectives:

This directly contributes to alleviating energy cost burdens on consumers and, in places like Curaçao, can help combat energy poverty. (Notably, about 25% of Curaçao’s population lives below the poverty line, and high utility bills exacerbate financial hardship.)

* **Demand Peak Reduction:** Flatten the building’s load profile to reduce evening peak demand. Lower peaks benefit the grid by reducing strain and the need for expensive peaking power plants. For utilities, this means improved grid stability and deferred infrastructure upgrades. For example, our simulations show the EMS can cut household peak grid import by a substantial margin, contributing to a more stable grid (detailed in Results).

* **Renewable Energy Integration:** Increase the consumption of locally generated renewable energy (especially rooftop PV) by intelligent timing of flexible loads. This boosts the effective value of renewables. In testing, the EMS raised PV self-consumption from roughly 42% to 87% for a solar-equipped home, meaning far less solar energy was wasted or sold back at low rates. This supports sustainability goals and reduces dependence on fossil fuels. In Curaçao, where over 95% of electricity is still from petroleum fuels, improving renewable utilization is particularly valuable.

* **User Engagement and Comfort:** Provide a user-centric design where residents remain in control of their appliance preferences and comfort settings. The EMS should operate mostly in the background, but with transparency and easy overrides. One aim is to include budgeting tools and feedback that increase **energy awareness**. By seeing how and when they use energy (and what it costs), users can make more informed decisions. This is part of tackling energy poverty through behavior—if people can understand their consumption patterns, they can adjust them with the EMS’s help.

* **Strategic Flexibility for Utilities:** Demonstrate how utilities could leverage such an EMS for broader programs. For instance, if many homes have EMS, a utility could introduce innovative tariffs (like critical peak pricing or real-time pricing) knowing customers have tools to respond. It also opens the door for **aggregated demand response**, where the utility or a third-party can send signals to many EMS-equipped homes to curtail load during system stress events. The EMS architecture was designed to easily integrate such signals (e.g., via an API) in the future. This creates strategic value in evolving the customer-utility relationship from one-way consumption to interactive participation in energy markets.

* **Adaptability to Emerging Markets:** Ensure the system can be adapted to environments like Curaçao’s, by building in flexibility for different pricing schemes, data availability, and infrastructure. In practice, this means the EMS can work with coarser or less frequent data, operate with or without internet connectivity continuously, and use defaults or simulations to fill data gaps during the transition period. This adaptability is a strategic design value: it generalizes the solution so it’s not locked to one country’s system but can be **localized** to others with minimal changes.

In summary, the EMS project is strategically positioned to deliver a **win-win-win**: end-users save money and gain comfort, utilities get a more flexible and stable grid, and society benefits from lower emissions and progress toward energy equity. These align with broader policy goals: for instance, the European Union’s push for demand-side management and the Caribbean’s goals for energy independence and resilience. By quantifying benefits (like cost savings percentage, peak reduction, etc.) and addressing practical deployment issues, this project adds concrete evidence that intelligent energy management is a worthwhile investment.

### 3.3 Energy Landscape: Netherlands vs. Curaçao

The Netherlands and Curaçao offer a study in contrasts regarding energy systems, which directly influences how an EMS operates in each.

**Netherlands Context:** The Netherlands has a highly developed and liberalized energy market. Most households can opt for dynamic pricing contracts where electricity prices change hourly based on wholesale markets. Smart meter penetration is high (over 80% of Dutch homes have smart meters), enabling detailed usage tracking and two-way communication. Renewable energy, especially wind and solar, has grown rapidly; on certain days, a substantial portion of electricity comes from renewables. However, this also means price volatility – for example, midday prices can drop very low when solar production is high, and evening prices can spike when demand is high but solar is offline. The grid is reliable and outages are rare, so the EMS in the Netherlands mainly focuses on economic optimization (taking advantage of price swings) and aiding the grid through demand response. Energy poverty is a concern as prices increased: as noted earlier, the 2022 crisis pushed many households into difficulty paying bills, reinforcing interest in solutions that can reduce or shift consumption. Dutch consumers are also environmentally conscious; increasing self-consumption of solar via an EMS can improve one’s **sustainability profile** (e.g., using more of your own green energy instead of drawing from the grid).

**Curaçao Context:** Curaçao is an island with a small population (\~150k) and an isolated grid. Electricity generation is dominated by imported fossil fuels (diesel or heavy fuel oil), which historically made electricity expensive (around \$0.26 per kWh in 2015, which is actually slightly below the Caribbean regional average of \$0.33, but far above typical U.S. or European prices). There are two wind farms and some distributed solar, contributing roughly 15–25% of energy in recent years, but the rest is oil-based generation. The utility (Aqualectra) is vertically integrated and until recently had a monopoly; there is interest in allowing independent power producers, but that’s still in progress. The pricing system in Curaçao is currently a monthly flat rate (with perhaps slight variations or fuel clauses), meaning consumers pay roughly the same rate at all times of day. Smart metering and real-time pricing are not yet implemented, though plans exist to modernize the grid and possibly introduce more granular tariffs. The implication is that today, an EMS in Curaçao would not optimize for price differences (there are none), but it can still optimize for **operational efficiency** (for example, scheduling to match solar production or to stay under certain demand thresholds if the tariff has tiers). More importantly, the EMS sets the stage for **future** dynamic pricing: as the island moves to smarter grid tech, the EMS can directly leverage any new pricing schemes (the system is built with that flexibility in mind). Another critical difference is infrastructure reliability; island grids can experience outages if generation fails or during extreme weather. An EMS with storage can help a building ride through outages by prioritizing critical loads, effectively improving resilience – a use-case less needed in the Netherlands but very relevant in Curaçao’s context of hurricane risks and older grid assets.

**Cultural and Usage Pattern Differences:** In the Netherlands, peak residential usage often occurs on winter evenings (due to heating, lighting, cooking), whereas Curaçao’s peak might be more related to air conditioning on hot days (year-round summer). This affects how the EMS schedules loads – e.g., in Curaçao pre-cooling a building before the hottest part of the day when solar is plentiful could be a strategy, whereas in the Netherlands pre-heating in mid-afternoon before a peak price evening might be useful. The EMS’s machine learning models can capture these patterns given appropriate training data. However, initially, since Curaçao data is limited, the models might rely on European proxies or generic appliance usage probabilities. Part of the project’s value is demonstrating that an EMS designed in one context can be **generalized** to another with appropriate localization (such as adjusting for different typical waking hours or appliance ownership rates).

In summary, the Netherlands provides the **testbed** with dynamic prices and data availability to develop the EMS’s core intelligence, and Curaçao provides the **target environment** where the EMS can create significant value by modernizing energy usage and mitigating high costs. Bridging these, the EMS is built to be **context-aware**: it can operate in a fully dynamic pricing mode or fall back to optimizing other metrics if only flat pricing is available (for instance, maximizing PV usage and enforcing user-set budget limits). This versatility ensures that as Curaçao (and similar regions) evolve their energy policies, the EMS can smoothly transition from today’s regime to tomorrow’s smart grid scenario.

## Literature Review

Research on Home and Building Energy Management Systems (HEMS/BEMS) has grown substantially in the past decade, reflecting the increasing importance of demand-side flexibility in modern grids. This section reviews key trends and findings from the literature, focusing on optimization techniques (especially MILP), uncertainty handling (robust and stochastic methods), and the integration of probabilistic modeling of demand.

**HEMS Optimization Approaches:** Early HEMS solutions often relied on heuristic or rule-based control (e.g., simple if-then rules to turn off appliances during peak hours) or user-driven scheduling via smartphone apps. While straightforward, those approaches cannot guarantee cost-optimal outcomes and often neglect the complex interactions between devices. In contrast, **optimization-based models** formulate the scheduling problem mathematically, capturing device constraints and cost objectives to find an optimal or near-optimal schedule. Among these, **Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP)** has emerged as a widely used method for HEMS due to its ability to handle binary on/off decisions and linear constraints efficiently. Many studies (e.g., Al-Sorour et al. 2022; Koltsaklis et al. 2019) have applied MILP to schedule home appliances under time-of-use or real-time pricing, showing significant cost savings compared to non-optimized usage. MILP is favored for its guarantee of finding a global optimum for the formulated problem and its flexibility to include various constraints (device operating limits, comfort requirements, etc.). For instance, one real-world case study in Portugal used a MILP-based model predictive control for a home with solar and battery, achieving better cost performance than both a pure MILP without feedback and the home’s original (unoptimized) operation. These results illustrate that MILP-based HEMS can indeed yield double-digit percentage savings in practice, validating our own approach.

However, a known drawback of MILP models is computational complexity, especially as problem size grows (many devices, fine time granularity, or multiple houses coordinated). MILP solve times can escalate in multi-building scenarios or with high-resolution time steps. Researchers have addressed this via two strategies: (1) **Decomposition and Hierarchical Control** – breaking the problem into smaller subproblems (e.g., solving per device or per sub-system, then a high-level coordinator aligns the solutions), or using rolling horizon (MPC) to limit the optimization window; and (2) **Hybrid Methods** – combining MILP with metaheuristics or machine learning to guide or approximate the solution. For example, integrating Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) with MILP has shown faster convergence for appliance scheduling under dynamic pricing. Such hybrid models may solve quicker but sometimes at the cost of a slight optimality gap. The literature suggests that for a single building with a handful of devices and hourly time steps, a well-formulated MILP can solve within seconds (as our results also confirm), whereas for coordinating dozens of homes or using 5-minute intervals, one must carefully consider scalability or use advanced solvers and computing resources.

**Robust and Stochastic Optimization:** A major challenge in HEMS is dealing with uncertainty – e.g., not knowing exactly when an occupant will need a flexible appliance or how much solar energy will be produced due to weather variability. Two principal frameworks exist: **robust optimization** and **stochastic (scenario-based) optimization**. Robust optimization seeks schedules that perform well under the worst-case realization of uncertainties. For instance, Mahmoudi et al. (2020) applied robust optimization to a HEMS problem, ensuring constraints are met for all possible values of uncertain parameters within predefined bounds. The advantage is high reliability (you won’t be caught off guard by a bad scenario), but it can be overly conservative – potentially sacrificing cost savings to hedge against rare extremes. A recent study by Tostado-Véliz et al. (2023) proposed a *fully robust* HEMS model considering uncertainties in real-time prices, demand, and electric vehicle (EV) availability. Their model applies robust optimization to *all* uncertainties with adjustable conservatism and showed it’s tractable with modern solvers. This underscores that robust methods can be viable for HEMS, especially with one or two major uncertain factors like EV usage, but complexity grows with more uncertainties.

Stochastic optimization, on the other hand, incorporates multiple scenarios (and their probabilities) to optimize an expected cost or a risk measure. Instead of one worst-case, many possible futures are simulated. Yoshida et al. (2018) demonstrated stochastic programming for a home with renewable generation. More recently, two-stage stochastic models have been used: e.g., one stage decides day-ahead commitments, and the second stage adjusts to actual realization (similar to our two-phase approach but from a mathematical optimization perspective). In one example, authors formulated scenarios for wind power output and EV availability, then solved a two-stage MILP minimizing the expected cost over those scenarios. Such models can explicitly balance cost versus risk by including numerous scenarios including extreme ones. The downside is potentially large model size (more scenarios means more variables and constraints). Some works combine robust and stochastic ideas – for instance, using *distributionally robust optimization* which optimizes against the worst distribution within an ambiguity set, or hybridizing stochastic programming with robust constraints on certain critical variables. A scenario-based **robust** approach was demonstrated in an energy management context by considering a set of representative scenarios and optimizing for worst-case performance among them, effectively controlling downside risk without overly penalizing all outcomes.

**Probabilistic Modeling and Prediction:** Rather than treating device usage as an unknown to hedge against, another body of research focuses on predicting or learning usage patterns. Machine learning techniques, from simple time-of-use statistical models to advanced deep learning, have been applied to forecast demand or appliance use. For example, Neural Networks and LightGBM models can predict next-day load curves or the probability an appliance will be used in each hour. Our approach with a **Probability Model Agent** aligns with these trends – it learns probability mass functions (PMFs) for appliance usage from historical data. This is related to concepts in some studies where **occupant behavior modeling** is incorporated into HEMS. Chen et al. (2024) highlight AI-driven control that takes into account user habits. By learning when a user typically runs the washing machine or how often the EV is plugged in, the EMS can schedule more intelligently (e.g., if the user usually doesn’t run the dryer at 2am, the EMS might schedule it at 2am only if the probability is low but the cost benefit is very high, and even then possibly notify the user because it’s unusual).

Recent literature (Rehman et al. 2025) emphasizes that despite progress, **integrating user behavior and preferences** is still an underexplored area. Our work contributes to this by explicitly integrating learned probabilistic user preferences into the optimization objective (via a penalty for scheduling outside typical times). This acts somewhat like a “soft constraint” for comfort. Other researchers have used techniques like **chance constraints**, where a constraint (e.g., “dishwashing must be done by 7pm”) is allowed to be violated with a small probability. This can be more flexible than a hard deadline constraint. Although we did not explicitly use chance constraints, our probabilistic cost term serves a similar purpose by guiding the optimizer toward high-probability times.

**Comparative Insights:** Many studies report significant cost savings or peak reductions with their HEMS implementations, but the magnitudes vary widely depending on assumptions. A review of recent papers shows savings from \~10% up to \~40% in various scenarios, which match well with what we found (roughly 12–38% range in our tests). When energy storage is added, some works note even higher benefits, though one must account for battery degradation costs. (Our inclusion of a degradation cost coefficient follows best practices seen in literature to avoid optimizing battery use in a way that would prematurely wear it out.) One particularly relevant domain of research is peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading and community energy management. For example, some recent models optimize not just within one home but across a community, allowing houses to trade energy. While that is beyond our current scope, our architecture could extend to a community EMS with a higher-level coordinator (and we even did a simulation of coordinating 6 buildings, see Results). The literature shows P2P trading can further improve renewable utilization and cost distribution, which is a logical next step for future work.

In summary, the literature underlines that **MILP remains a strong baseline** for HEMS optimization, often enhanced by stochastic/robust techniques to cope with uncertainty and by machine learning to forecast and personalize control. Our EMS synthesizes these elements: MILP for optimal scheduling, scenario-based adjustments via a rolling horizon, robust-inspired safety margins in constraints (e.g., slightly underestimating solar to be safe), and ML for predictive modeling. This integrated approach positions our work at the forefront of current HEMS research. Furthermore, by focusing on an underserved context (Caribbean island systems and energy poverty considerations), our project adds a novel case study to the body of knowledge, which has largely focused on mainland grids and often overlooked the implementation challenges in developing regions.

## Project Ideation and Evolution

Large projects often undergo significant evolution from concept to completion, and this EMS project is no exception. In this section, we trace the **ideation journey**, highlighting how initial ideas were refined or pivoted in response to practical considerations, and justify the architectural and methodological choices made along the way.

**Initial Concept – The Digital Twin Approach:** The project began with the idea of creating a comprehensive **digital twin** of a building’s energy system. The notion was to simulate a virtual replica of a house or building, including its appliances, electrical infrastructure, and possibly even occupant behavior. This digital twin would allow experimentation with control strategies (like a sandbox) before implementing them in reality. The motivation was that a high-fidelity simulation environment could be used to test various algorithms (rule-based, optimization-based, etc.) under dynamic pricing scenarios to see which yields the best results. Early project proposals envisioned developing this simulation alongside a basic scheduling algorithm, with the digital twin serving as a proof-of-concept to demonstrate potential savings to stakeholders.

**From Simulation to Optimization:** As the team delved deeper, it became apparent that while a digital twin is useful for testing, the core value of the project would lie in the **optimization logic** that actually drives cost savings. In other words, simulating a building without significantly improving its operation would not meet the ambitious goals. Moreover, developing a highly detailed simulation (with physics of HVAC systems, occupant stochastic models, etc.) could consume a lot of time and detract from building the real optimizer. We refocused on a more **analytical model** of the building: using historical data and simplified models (e.g., device power ratings and constraints) to inform the optimization, rather than an overly complex simulation. The project shifted to using existing tools (like OpenDSS or custom Python simulations) in a more targeted way for evaluation only, and concentrating efforts on designing the EMS algorithms themselves.

**Adopting a Probabilistic Optimization Strategy:** A major evolution was the decision to incorporate **probabilistic modeling of appliance usage** directly into the optimization engine. Initially, we considered treating appliance schedules as deterministic variables to optimize, with maybe a buffer for uncertainty (e.g., schedule laundry at 3pm assuming someone will load it by then). However, experiments and stakeholder feedback highlighted the risk of user discomfort: if the system picks an odd time to run an appliance just to save cost, but the user actually needed that appliance earlier, it fails the comfort criterion. Two possible solutions emerged in discussion: (1) a **digital twin with real-time simulation of user behavior** where the EMS could adjust on the fly (essentially an MPC that recedes every hour), or (2) incorporate a model of behavior into the planning itself. We chose the latter as it gives a more **principled mathematical foundation** – thus the concept of using learned probability distributions (PMFs) for each device’s usage times. The MILP was extended to include a term in the objective that penalizes schedules unlikely to match user behavior (softly, rather than a hard rule). This approach was influenced by ideas from recent literature where stochastic programming or chance constraints handle uncertain loads, but here we directly embed probabilities into a single-stage optimizer for simplicity and transparency. This design choice was a key milestone: it meant our EMS would not just be optimizing an abstract cost, but effectively **co-optimizing cost and convenience**.

**Architecture Decision – Agent-Based Modular Design:** Initially, the team considered building the EMS as a monolithic application – one program that takes all inputs (prices, data, etc.) and outputs device schedules. While simpler at first glance, that approach raised concerns about scalability and maintainability. Drawing inspiration from industrial control systems and multi-agent systems literature, we opted for an **agent-based architecture**. Each functional element (prediction, scheduling, device control, etc.) would be an independent agent/module with well-defined interfaces. This decision was justified by several reasons (as also enumerated in Section 6.4): modularity for easier testing, scalability to add new devices or optimization strategies, and separation of concerns (e.g., isolating the learning algorithm from the MILP solver, so each can evolve independently). For example, during development we found it useful that one team member could improve the **ProbabilityModelAgent** (tweaking the learning algorithm for appliance usage) while another worked on the **GlobalOptimizer** MILP formulation – with a clear contract of data exchange between them. This parallel development validated the agent approach. Additionally, when integrating with external systems (like a utility price API or a building management system), having a dedicated **Integration Layer** agent meant those external calls could be managed without cluttering the core optimization code.

**Technology Stack and Tools:** The project evaluated several tools for optimization and machine learning. Early on, there was a question: should we use a commercial MILP solver (like Gurobi or CPLEX) for maximum speed and advanced features, or stick to open-source (like CBC via PuLP) to ensure the solution is affordable and extensible by the utility partner? We chose the open-source route using **PuLP with CBC** solver, for now, to align with the idea of a **production-ready, low-cost solution**. This did limit performance slightly, but as mentioned, our problem sizes were manageable (and we kept the option to plug in Gurobi for larger cases if needed). For machine learning, we tried a variety of algorithms for classification of appliance usage (logistic regression, random forests, etc.) and found gradient boosting models (**LightGBM, CatBoost**) offered the best accuracy and handled categorical time features well. These were integrated into the ProbabilityModelAgent. An evolution here was the adoption of **MLflow** for tracking experiments – initially not planned, but introduced to systematically log model versions and optimization runs, which proved valuable especially when demonstrating improvements to stakeholders (we could show how a newer model version improved AUC or how a newer MILP version reduced cost further, with full traceability).

**Feature Cut – Demand Forecasting vs. Price Response:** In early brainstorming, we considered implementing not just price-response optimization but also **demand forecasting** for the building’s inflexible load (like predicting the baseline HVAC or plug load). The idea was that if you can predict the non-shiftable part of the load, you can schedule the flexible part around it better. However, as the project progressed, the scope was tightened and we deprioritized explicit baseline forecasting, partly because the MILP can incorporate historical average non-flex load as a given profile, and any errors there would somewhat net out when recomputed daily. Instead, we focused on forecasting specifically for PV generation (since solar output significantly affects the optimization). This focus shift was due to time constraints and the fact that building a top-notch forecasting model for general loads could be a full project by itself. We leveraged existing datasets and simple models to estimate PV and non-flex load profiles, sufficient for our optimization needs.

**Pilot and Feedback Loop:** As a living lab project, we had the opportunity to discuss interim results with both academic supervisors and industry partners (utility reps). Early simulations with a digital twin showed the concept was promising but stakeholders asked: *“How do we know this will work with actual users? What if people simply don’t want a machine controlling their appliances?”* This feedback led to an increased emphasis on the **user interface and override features**. We added a **User Interface Layer** agent and sketched out dashboard ideas: users could set preferences (e.g., “latest wash cycle finish time = 9pm”) and see what the EMS scheduled. This human-in-the-loop consideration wasn’t prominent in the first concept but became important later. We realized that to gain user trust, transparency (like showing “By moving your AC usage by 1 hour, you saved \$2 today”) and easy control are crucial. While a full UI implementation was beyond the prototype, the architecture allows it, and we made sure the scheduling algorithm could take simple user inputs (like preferred time windows) as constraints or penalties.

**From Single-Building to Multi-Building (and back):** At one point, we explored extending the EMS to coordinate a **cluster of buildings** (like a community microgrid). A simulation scenario was developed to optimize 6 houses together versus individually (essentially adding a top-layer optimization that could trade energy between buildings if allowed). This was partly inspired by literature on community energy management and peer-to-peer trading. However, implementing a full multi-building optimizer introduced complexity and was not a direct requirement of our stakeholders. We included a **proof-of-concept** test in our evaluation (see Results: Cross-Building Coordination) to show that our EMS could be a building block in a future community-level system, but we pulled back to focusing on the single-building case for the main development. The project timeline meant we needed to deliver a solid solution for one building rather than a half-solution for many. The architecture, fortunately, is such that running multiple EMS instances with a higher-level coordinator is feasible later if desired.

In conclusion, the project evolved from a broad, simulation-heavy concept to a **focused, optimization-centric prototype**. Key decisions like embedding probabilistic learning, using an agent-based design, and maintaining user-centric features were driven by a mix of practical constraints, stakeholder input, and academic insight. Each decision was evaluated on how it impacts the final objectives: cost savings, robustness, and deployability. The end result is a system that we believe strikes the right balance between **academic novelty** (in methodology) and **industrial relevance** (in solving real problems and being implementable). The lessons learned during this evolution – such as the importance of flexibility and the need for continuous learning – are themselves valuable outcomes that can guide similar projects in the future.

## System Architecture

The EMS is built on a **modular, multi-layer architecture** that separates concerns and allows scalability. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level design (each box represents a module or layer and arrows indicate data/control flows). At the highest level, the system is organized into **five layers**, each responsible for a specific set of functions:

* **Data Layer:** This bottom layer handles all data acquisition, cleansing, storage, and basic retrieval. It ingests data from sensors and external sources (smart meters, PV inverters, weather APIs, etc.) and ensures consistency and time-synchronization. Data is stored in a local database (we use DuckDB for fast analytical queries on time-series data) with a well-defined schema. This layer abstracts away raw data intricacies and provides a clean interface (through queries or APIs) for the rest of the system. For example, a higher layer can request “get last 7 days of load data for device X” without knowing where or how it’s stored.

* **Model Layer:** This layer hosts all predictive models and analytics. It includes the machine learning models that forecast device usage probabilities, solar generation, etc. Essentially, it is the “brain” that learns patterns from historical data. In our implementation, this includes LightGBM and CatBoost models for classification of device usage in each hour, as well as a simple time-series model for PV output using weather forecasts. The models in this layer output insights like: *the probability the dishwasher is started in each hour of the day is \[0%,0%,…20% at 7pm,…]* or *predicted solar output tomorrow at noon is 3 kW*. These outputs then feed into the optimization decisions. By isolating the Model layer, we ensure that the system’s intelligence (learning) can be upgraded or replaced independently (for example, switching to a neural network model later would not affect other layers as long as the interface remains the same).

* **Optimization Layer:** This is the core decision-making layer that produces the optimal schedule for devices. It implements the mathematical optimization logic, which in our case is the MILP formulation. The **GlobalOptimizer** agent resides here as the central coordinator that runs the MILP solver, incorporating data and model predictions from lower layers. The optimization layer has knowledge of all controllable resources – flexible device schedules, battery charging/discharging, and so on – and their constraints. When triggered (e.g., once a day after new prices and forecasts are available), it formulates the MILP problem and computes the schedule (e.g., “EV charges from 2am-4am, washing machine at 11am,” etc.). It also can run quick re-optimizations intraday if needed (though in our design, intraday adjustments are handled by simpler heuristics or by recomputing the next day plan gradually incorporating new data).

* **Integration Layer:** This layer manages external communications and integration points. It handles interactions with the broader energy ecosystem: for instance, calling an API to fetch day-ahead price data, sending control signals to smart plugs or a building management system (BMS), and interfacing with cloud services. In our architecture, the Integration Layer includes modules like an **API Gateway** (for RESTful API endpoints), a message broker for internal asynchronous communication, and any authentication/security services needed to protect data exchange. By segregating integration, we can deploy the EMS in different environments (cloud vs on-premise) and connect to different utilities or devices by swapping out only this layer’s configurations or plugins.

* **User Interface (UI) Layer:** The top layer provides interfaces for end-users and facility managers to interact with the EMS. This could be a web dashboard, mobile app, or even voice assistant integration. The UI layer presents data (current energy usage, today’s schedule, cost savings achieved, etc.) and accepts user inputs (like override commands or preference settings). In our prototype, we implemented a simple web dashboard showing device schedules and allowing the user to modify preferences like “don’t run dryer after 10pm”. This layer is crucial for user acceptance – it turns the EMS from a black-box optimizer into a user-cooperative system. It communicates with the Integration and Optimization layers to get data and send user commands down.

Crucially, our architecture is **agent-based**: within these layers, specific **agents** encapsulate functionality. For example, we have:

* **GlobalOptimizer Agent:** (Optimization Layer) – the MILP solver orchestrator. It collects inputs (price, PV forecast, device usage probabilities, etc.) and runs the optimization. It’s aware of all devices and coordinates scheduling among them.

* **ProbabilityModelAgent:** (Model Layer) – continuously learns device usage patterns from historical data. Each day it updates the probability distributions (using Bayesian updates or machine learning) for each flexible device’s usage profile. It might use metrics like **Jensen-Shannon divergence** to gauge if today’s usage deviated from predictions and adjust accordingly (this was implemented in our training loop to progressively refine the model).

* **FlexibleDeviceAgent:** (could be seen as part of Optimization Layer or its own layer) – manages a specific flexible appliance’s state and constraints. We conceptualized each major flexible device (e.g., washing machine, dishwasher) as having an agent that knows its requirements: cycle duration, earliest start, latest finish, etc. These agents report their status and constraints to the GlobalOptimizer and can execute the on/off commands it returns. This encapsulation means adding a new appliance type (say a pool pump) would involve introducing a new agent for it with appropriate constraints.

* **BatteryAgent / EVAgent:** (Optimization Layer) – specialized agents for energy storage devices (stationary battery or EV battery). They handle battery-specific logic like State of Charge (SoC) tracking, ensuring SoC stays within limits, handling charging efficiency, and applying degradation costs. The EVAgent extends BatteryAgent by also considering the vehicle’s schedule (must be charged by departure time, possibly even vehicle-to-grid dispatch if allowed).

* **PVAgent:** (Model Layer) – responsible for solar generation predictions and possibly curtailment control. It takes weather data (irradiance forecasts, etc.) and produces a PV output forecast curve, including uncertainty ranges. It shares this with the optimizer so that the MILP can plan using expected PV production and, if needed, scenario analyses for PV uncertainty.

* **GridAgent:** (Integration Layer) – encapsulates the grid tariffs and constraints. It provides the price data to the system and encodes grid import/export rules (for instance, in our test, we assumed import price €0.25/kWh and feed-in (export) price €0.05/kWh, plus a limit on reverse power flow if any). The GridAgent can also be where demand response signals or grid requests are handled in the future.

These agents interact through well-defined interfaces, often via an internal event bus or broker. For example, each day at 5pm, the GridAgent posts an event “New prices available for next 24h”; the GlobalOptimizer, waiting for that, then requests data from PVAgent and ProbabilityModelAgent (“give me tomorrow’s PV forecast” and “give me latest usage probability distributions”), then runs the MILP optimization. Once a schedule is computed, it informs each FlexibleDeviceAgent and BatteryAgent of their operation plan. During operation, if a user manually turns on a device out-of-schedule, the relevant device agent can notify the GlobalOptimizer or ProbabilityModelAgent to update its model (learning that the user deviated from plan at that time).

A key architectural decision to highlight is **why an agent-based approach**. We considered the complexity overhead of multiple agents vs. a simpler unified program. The benefits we found and align with control systems theory include modularity, easier testing (each agent can be unit-tested with simulated inputs), and resilience. If one agent fails (say the PV forecast service goes down), the system can degrade gracefully (GlobalOptimizer could use last known forecast or a default), rather than a monolithic system potentially crashing entirely. This also aids in meeting **production-quality standards** such as maintainability and traceability. Each agent was implemented with logs and, where feasible, self-checks (for instance, BatteryAgent ensuring it never schedules beyond its SoC limits, etc.). This aligns with **separation of concerns** in software design and ensures that as the system grows (new devices, more buildings, etc.), we can extend it without rewriting core components.

Security and privacy are also considered in the architecture. The Integration Layer’s API gateway ensures that any external commands or data queries are authenticated. Data Layer could enforce encryption at rest if needed for sensitive data like user habits. Because the EMS might deal with private information (when people use appliances can infer occupancy patterns), we included privacy in the design requirements (though the specifics are more in implementation details).

In essence, the system architecture provides a **scaffold that balances flexibility and control**. It is flexible in that new modules or agents can be added (imagine a “WeatherAgent” for more advanced climate impact modeling, or a “CommunityAgent” to coordinate multiple EMS instances) without altering existing ones. It offers control by structuring interactions – for example, only the Optimization Layer makes decisions, the Model Layer only informs, and the UI Layer only requests or overrides within allowed bounds. This clear layering and agent responsibility proved invaluable in keeping the system understandable and extensible. The architecture set the stage for the methodology described next, wherein we detail how data flows through these layers and agents to achieve the EMS’s objectives.

## Methodology

This section details our technical approach to realizing the EMS functionality, from data processing and machine learning to the MILP optimization and how we handle uncertainties. In summary, the EMS methodology follows a **pipeline of steps** each day: data preprocessing, probabilistic modeling of usage, day-ahead MILP optimization, and then continuous learning/updating as actual data comes in. We also describe how we integrate these steps with tools like MLflow for experiment tracking and how robust or scenario-based techniques are incorporated.

### 7.1 Probabilistic Device Modeling and Data Analysis

The first part of the methodology is making sense of the historical data to inform future decisions. We gather data such as: past appliance usage logs (timestamps when each appliance was run), historical electricity consumption curves, weather (for PV), and any relevant contextual info (day of week, holidays, etc.). **Data preprocessing** involves cleaning this data: handling missing values, synchronizing time stamps (we use a 1-hour time step for optimization, so we aggregate higher-frequency smart meter data into hourly totals), and labeling data where needed. For example, we tag each hour for each device as “usage” or “no usage” based on sensing or smart plug data, building a binary time series per device.

From this processed data, we perform exploratory analysis to identify patterns. We often find that certain devices have strong daily or weekly patterns. For instance, washing machines might show peaks on Saturday mornings (when people do laundry on weekends), EV charging might have peaks at 6pm (after work arrival). Understanding these patterns is crucial. We calculate statistics like average usage start time, frequency of use per day, etc., and use these to set reasonable bounds in the MILP (e.g., a device that is used at most once a day in history we will model with a once-per-day constraint).

We then train machine learning models to predict **Probability Mass Functions (PMFs)** for device usage. Concretely, for each flexible device we define \$P\_{d,t}\$ as the probability device \$d\$ is started in hour \$t\$ of a generic day. We choose a 24-hour cycle resolution for these probabilities (so each device has 24 probabilities summing to 1). To compute these, one approach is simply taking historical frequency: if out of 30 recorded days, a dishwasher started 15 times at 7pm, then \$P\_{\text{dishwasher},19:00} = 0.5\$. We improve on this by using features: we know usage might depend on context (e.g., EV charging probability is higher on weekdays at 6pm if the person commutes, but lower on weekends). So we train, for each device, a classifier that given the context (day type, previous usage, etc.) outputs a probability of usage for each hour. In practice, we implemented a lightweight approach where for each upcoming day we first classify it as a “weekday” or “weekend/holiday”, then use a historical profile for that category. Additionally, we have an **adaptive Bayesian update** mechanism: each day, after observing actual usage, we update the probabilities for the next day. This is akin to having a prior distribution (initial PMF from training) and then adjusting it with recent evidence, giving more weight to recent patterns while not forgetting long-term trends. Mathematically, if \$P\_{d,t}\$ was yesterday’s probability distribution and we observed usage at time \$h\$, we nudge the distribution to increase \$P\_{d,h}\$ for future, using a learning rate parameter. Appendix D details the hyperparameters for this updating (e.g., learning rate, decay of old data).

The output of this step is, for each flexible device \$d\$, a vector \${P\_{d,0}, P\_{d,1}, ..., P\_{d,23}}\$ representing the likelihood of it being used in each hour. This is a central piece of input to the optimization.

### 7.2 MILP Optimization Formulation

At the heart of the EMS is the **Mixed-Integer Linear Programming** formulation that computes the optimal schedule. We formulate a day-ahead optimization for a horizon of 24 hours (which can be extended to 48 hours if needed to avoid end effects, but in our case 24 with an end condition works). The MILP decides on binary variables for appliance operation and continuous variables for battery charging, etc., to minimize a cost function.

**Decision Variables:** We define binary variables \$x\_{d,t}\$ which equal 1 if flexible appliance \$d\$ is running (or started, depending on modeling) in hour \$t\$, and 0 otherwise. Some appliances like washers have a multi-hour cycle – for those we actually model with start variables \$y\_{d,t}\$ (1 if starts at \$t\$) and derive \$x\_{d,t}\$ from those (meaning if a washing machine starts at hour 5 and has a 2-hour cycle, then \$x\_{d,5}=x\_{d,6}=1\$ based on one start variable \$y\_{d,5}=1\$). We also have continuous variables: \$b^+\_t\$ for battery charge power in hour \$t\$, \$b^-\_t\$ for battery discharge power in hour \$t\$. Similarly, \$g^+\_t\$ and \$g^-\_t\$ could denote grid import and export power. We constrain those based on power limits and battery state of charge.

**Objective Function:** The objective is to minimize total **operational cost** over the day. This cost has multiple components:

* **Energy Cost from Grid:** \$\sum\_{t} p\_t \cdot g^+\_t\$ minus any revenue from export \$\sum\_t p\_t^\text{export} \cdot g^-\_t\$. In our formulation we actually combine these where possible, but essentially importing costs money at price \$p\_t\$ and exporting yields money at a (usually lower) price.
* **Battery Degradation Cost:** We include a term \$p^{\text{deg}} \cdot (b^+\_t + b^-\_t)\$ summing over \$t\$, where \$p^{\text{deg}}\$ is a cost coefficient (€/kWh throughput) reflecting battery wear-and-tear. This was set to a small value like €0.045/kWh based on battery cycle life data (so roughly €45 per MWh cycled, which spreads the battery replacement cost over its expected throughput).
* **User Discomfort Penalty:** This is our novel term derived from the probabilities. For each device \$d\$, we add a cost \$w\_{prob,d} \cdot \sum\_{t}(1 - P\_{d,t}) \cdot x\_{d,t}\$ in the objective. Here \$w\_{prob,d}\$ is a weight (tunable parameter reflecting how much we care about preference vs cost). If the optimizer schedules device \$d\$ at time \$t\$, it pays a “penalty” proportional to \$(1 - P\_{d,t})\$ – so scheduling at a time with 90% probability of usage gives a small 0.1 penalty, whereas scheduling at a time with only 10% probability gives a 0.9 penalty (thus discouraged unless price savings are huge). This formulation effectively pushes schedules toward high-likelihood times for user behavior, maintaining comfort.

In equation form, our objective can be summarized as:

$\min \sum_{t=0}^{23} \Big[ p_t \cdot (g^+_t - g^-_t) + p^{\text{deg}} \cdot (b^+_t + b^-_t) + \sum_{d \in \text{devices}} w_{prob,d} \cdot (1 - P_{d,t}) \cdot x_{d,t} \Big].$

The optimizer will trade off these components. If, say, electricity price \$p\_t\$ is very high at 7pm and low at 3am, it will lean toward running devices at 3am – but if \$P\_{d,3\text{am}}\$ is zero (household usually asleep), it incurs a penalty. Depending on \$w\_{prob,d}\$, it might decide maybe 3am is not worth it and choose a moderately higher price time when \$P\$ is higher (like 6am if early risers, etc.). These weights can be tuned; in testing we found a sweet spot where cost savings are still substantial but user preference satisfaction stays >85%. In effect, we shaped \$w\_{prob,d}\$ so that the model schedules outside normal hours only if the price difference is very significant.

**Constraints:** We have several sets of constraints in the MILP:

* **Device Operation Constraints:** For each flexible appliance:

  * **Cycle integrity:** If a device runs for \$r\_d\$ hours once started, we enforce that if it starts at \$t\$ (\$y\_{d,t}=1\$) then \$x\_{d,t}\$ through \$x\_{d,t+r\_d-1}\$ are 1, and we prevent overlapping cycles.
  * **One start per day (for certain devices):** \$\sum\_t y\_{d,t} \le 1\$ if the device can only run once a day (or some max number for others).
  * **Energy requirement:** For flexible loads that consume a fixed total energy \$E\_d\$ but can be spread (like an HVAC that must deliver X kWh of cooling over the day, or EV charging that needs \$E\_d\$ total by departure), we have \$\sum\_t e\_{d,t} = E\_d\$ and limit \$e\_{d,t} \le P^{max}*d \cdot x*{d,t}\$. Here \$e\_{d,t}\$ is a continuous variable for energy consumed by device if using partial flexibility model.
  * **User-specified windows:** If a user says “not before 8am” or “finish by 9pm”, we fix \$x\_{d,t}=0\$ for forbidden times or ensure completion by deadline via appropriate sums. These are simple linear constraints.

* **Battery Constraints:** These include the battery state of charge (SoC) transitions and limits:
  $\text{SoC}_{t+1} = \text{SoC}_t + \eta_{\text{chg}} \cdot b^+_t - \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{dis}}} \cdot b^-_t,$ ensuring SoC updates with charging/discharging (with efficiency losses). We enforce SoC bounds: \$\text{SoC}\_{min} \le \text{SoC}*t \le \text{SoC}*{max}\$. Typically, SoC\_min might be 10% and SoC\_max 100% (or 90% if preserving battery life by not fully charging). Also, to avoid simultaneous charge and discharge: if we use binary indicators \$z^+\_t\$ and \$z^-\_t\$ for “charging mode” or “discharging mode”, we say \$b^+\_t \le P^{\max} z^+\_t\$, \$b^-\_t \le P^{\max} z^-\_t\$, and \$z^+*t + z^-*t \le 1\$ (so not both at once). This avoids non-physical solutions like charging and discharging at same time to arbitrage incentives.
  We also include an optional constraint for EV: SoC at departure hour must be at least the required level (if departure at 7am, SoC\_7am = 100% or whatever target). This is implemented simply by fixing \$\text{SoC}*{dep} = \text{SoC}*{\max}\$ or adding a soft penalty if not reached (depending on user choice for EV readiness).

* **Energy Balance and Grid Limits:** We link device usage, battery, PV, and grid via an energy balance equation each hour:
  $g^+_t - g^-_t = \sum_{d \in devices} P_{d}^{use} \cdot x_{d,t} + b^+_t - b^-_t - s_t,$
  where \$s\_t\$ is PV generation (treated as a negative load, known from forecast, or a decision if curtailment allowed). This equation says: grid import minus export equals net demand (sum of all device consumptions plus any battery charge minus discharges minus local solar). We set bounds on grid import/export: e.g., \$0 \le g^+\_t \le G^{\max}\$, \$0 \le g^-*t \le G^{\max}*{\text{export}}\$. In a single home, these limits might be high enough to never bind (other than non-negativity), unless the grid connection has a capacity limit. In some scenario tests, we tried a limit on \$g^+\$ to simulate avoiding peaks.

The MILP as formulated is solved once per day with inputs:

* Price vector \$p\_t\$ (and export price vector if separate).
* PV generation forecast \$s\_t\$ for each hour (we usually treat it as deterministic in the day-ahead plan, but see Uncertainty section for how we tested errors).
* Device probabilities \$P\_{d,t}\$ and constraints for each device.
* Initial battery SoC (we take end of previous day’s SoC as input).
* The objective weights \$w\_{prob,d}\$, \$p^{deg}\$ etc., configured beforehand.

The output is a schedule: values for \$x\_{d,t}\$ (or \$y\_{d,t}\$ start times) for each appliance and power setpoints \$b^+\_t, b^-\_t\$ for the battery, for each hour.

We implemented this MILP using PuLP in Python, which translates to an LP file and solved with CBC. The solve time on average was around 8.2 seconds per building per day, which is very acceptable for day-ahead use (and even if we did hourly re-planning, it’s fine). If needed, the MILP could be sped up by using commercial solvers or more computing power, but part of our methodology was to prove that even open-source tools suffice for a single building optimization problem of this size.

### 7.3 Uncertainty Handling and Robust Optimization

While the MILP above assumes a certain set of inputs each day, reality can deviate. We incorporated several methods to handle uncertainty in both the design of the MILP and the operational use of its results:

* **Probabilistic Constraints (Soft):** The aforementioned probability-weighted cost in the objective is one way we handle uncertainty in user behavior. Instead of treating a device’s use time as fixed or worst-case, we allow it to be scheduled but charge a penalty if that schedule is statistically “unlikely.” This effectively means the optimizer won’t heavily commit to a schedule that is likely to be wrong (like running something at an odd hour), providing a cushion. It’s a form of robust optimization in a soft sense – we don’t guarantee 100% that the user will follow the schedule, but we bias the solution to align with expected behavior. In robust optimization terms, we’re optimizing for a scenario that is “most probable” rather than the absolute worst, which is a middle ground approach.

* **Scenario Analysis in Simulation:** Although our daily optimization is single-scenario (using expected values), we perform scenario analysis outside of it. In the simulation framework (Section 8), we tested how the EMS performs under different scenarios: e.g., *What if the next day’s prices are 50% more volatile than historically?* or *What if the PV forecast is 20% off?*. We didn’t implement a multi-scenario MILP (which would be a stochastic programming approach) because of complexity, but we mimic its effect by running the deterministic MILP with a range of input variations and observing outcomes. If a certain scenario (like much higher peak price) causes issues (say all devices pile into one cheap hour causing a new peak), we could adjust strategy, such as adding a **robust constraint** like “no more than N devices start in the same hour” to avoid that.

* **Reserve Margins:** In robust optimization, one often adds reserve margins to ensure feasibility under uncertainty. We applied a similar idea to PV integration: given a PV forecast, we scale it down a bit (e.g., use 90% of predicted output in the MILP) to hedge against overestimation. This way, if clouds roll in and actual PV is less, the schedule is still mostly valid (worst case, battery discharges a bit more or grid supplies the shortfall). Another example: if an EV is scheduled to charge fully by 7am, we might target 6am in the plan (if the user allowed) as a robust measure so that even if there’s a power cut or delay, the EV still finishes by 7. These are heuristic robust measures but effective.

* **Continuous Re-optimization (Rolling Horizon):** Our EMS is not fire-and-forget after the day-ahead schedule. We employ a rolling horizon concept akin to Model Predictive Control (MPC). This means that as the day unfolds, we continuously update our information (via the ProbabilityModelAgent and real-time metering) and can adjust. In implementation, critical devices that haven’t started by their scheduled time raise a flag. For instance, if the schedule said dishwasher at 3pm but the user hasn’t loaded it by 3pm (no start observed), the FlexibleDeviceAgent can request a re-plan for that device (or simply skip it and reschedule next day). Similarly, if an appliance was unexpectedly started by the user at a different time, we update the model and could re-run the MILP for the remaining hours of the day with that device now running or with changed SoC, etc. We do this in a limited way (we didn’t fully implement 24 rolling MILPs per day, but the framework supports an update perhaps every hour or when major deviations occur).

* **Robust Constraints and Chance Constraints:** We experimented with adding some robust constraints to the MILP itself. One example: imposing that even if an appliance is not started as scheduled, the cost to user isn’t too high. This is tricky to encode, but effectively we made sure that no single appliance’s shift saves only a tiny amount of money (to avoid schedules that yield negligible benefit but high inconvenience). In robust optimization terms, we considered a constraint for worst-case user behavior: e.g., “if the user decides to override and use device at their preferred time instead, the cost difference isn’t huge.” This was more of a qualitative check than a strict constraint.

* **Two-Stage Decision Thinking:** Conceptually, our approach functions as a two-stage process: stage 1, schedule day-ahead (decisions before uncertainty realizes); stage 2, adjust during day (after uncertainty realizes). Although we do not formulate it explicitly as a two-stage stochastic program, we mimic that through the continuous learning and adjustment loop. If we were to formalize, the first stage decides start times, second stage could decide some real-time adjustments like minor time shifts or use of battery to compensate deviations.

One could extend our MILP to a robust MILP by including uncertainty sets for parameters (like demand or PV) and then using methods like cutting-plane generation for robustness. We chose a more straightforward approach given project time: forecast well, penalize improbable schedules, and adjust on the fly. This aligns with the trend in literature combining predictive controllers with optimization that re-solves frequently.

In summary, while our MILP is deterministic in formulation, the methodology around it – probabilistic inputs, penalty costs for unlikely events, and rolling adjustments – provides a **robustness layer** ensuring that the EMS operates reliably in the face of uncertainty, be it in human behavior or renewable fluctuations.

### 7.4 Implementation Details and Deployment Considerations

The EMS was implemented using a modern data science and optimization stack in Python. Key elements of the implementation include:

* **Programming Environment:** Python 3.9 was used, leveraging its rich ecosystem. We ensured the code was structured as a set of services (using Flask for the API, for example) to mirror the layered architecture. Agents were implemented as classes or processes depending on need. For instance, the GlobalOptimizer is a class with methods to run optimization, and it can be invoked via a scheduler (e.g., a cron job or an event trigger when new prices arrive).

* **PuLP & CBC Solver:** We used the PuLP library to formulate the MILP. PuLP provides a nice Pythonic interface to define decision variables, objective, and constraints, which we dynamically generate based on the devices present and the day’s data. CBC (Coin-or branch and cut) is the default open-source MILP solver we used. We compiled CBC to ensure it was the latest version. Solve times \~8 seconds as noted were observed on a machine with Intel Xeon CPU (we tested on a 4-core VM). If CBC couldn’t solve to optimality within 60 seconds (which happened in some multi-building tests), we would accept the best solution it had, but in single-building it usually found optimum quickly.

* **Machine Learning Libraries:** For the probability models, we used LightGBM and CatBoost libraries due to their efficiency and ability to handle categorical features like “hour of day” or “day of week” inherently. The training of these models (on historical data) was tracked in **MLflow**, which we set up to log each training run and resulting AUC or accuracy. This gave us a history of model performance as we tweaked features or parameters. The models are saved and loaded by the ProbabilityModelAgent for daily use.

* **MLflow & Logging:** Every optimization run (each day) and model update was logged using MLflow tracking. This means we record the version of the model used, the result schedule, and key metrics like “cost before vs after optimization” for that day, “PV utilization”, etc. Such logging is crucial for a production system to monitor that it’s actually delivering savings and not causing any issues. It also aids in debugging if on some day the solver behaves unexpectedly or a constraint was missed (we can replay that scenario).

* **Containerization:** The EMS services were containerized using Docker (one container for the main EMS logic, another for the database). This ensures that deploying on a server (or potentially on a local device like a Raspberry Pi, though that’s resource constrained) is straightforward. Containerization also helps isolate dependencies and manage updates.

* **Integration Points:** We implemented integration with an open API for day-ahead prices (in the Netherlands, EPEX spot prices can be fetched, or in our tests, we used an archive of them). For Curaçao, since dynamic prices are not there yet, we allowed the system to use a proxy price input or a manually set TOU tariff for testing. Integration with devices was simulated via a Home Assistant environment (in lab testing) – the EMS would output a schedule which we then applied to a Home Assistant instance controlling smart plugs. In a real deployment, this would be replaced by direct integration with smart appliances or the building management system (BMS) via standard protocols (we mention API, but protocols like MQTT or OpenADR could be used for demand response signals, etc.).

* **Security:** Because an EMS might be controlling critical loads, we enforced secure communication. API calls required a token, and we planned for encryption of any sensitive data. Container communication was internal but could be secured via network policies. While our prototype did not undergo a full security audit, we followed best practices (no hard-coded credentials, regular updates of libraries to patch vulnerabilities, etc.).

* **User Interface:** We built a simple web dashboard (using Dash/Plotly in Python) that shows a timeline of the day with blocks indicating when each device is scheduled to run, and a chart of forecast vs actual usage as the day progresses. The UI also had toggles to simulate user preferences (like enabling/disabling certain devices or setting comfort levels). This was not a fully polished product UI, but enough to demonstrate the concept. In deployment, a more refined UI or integration into existing energy management apps would be ideal.

* **Testing and Simulation:** A discrete-event simulation framework was developed to test the EMS under various scenarios before deployment. This simulation essentially acted as a digital twin (though simpler than the original “digital twin” concept): it took the EMS’s scheduled actions and “executed” them on a model of a building. If the EMS said EV charges at 2am-4am, the simulator would calculate the resulting SoC and grid draw, then if a real next day price scenario changed or a device was triggered by user, the simulator injects that event. This allowed us to compute metrics like total cost, peak demand, PV curtailment, etc., for each scenario. We simulated 7 prototypical buildings (5 houses with PV, EV, etc., 1 without PV, 1 small industrial) over a 90-day period to generate statistically robust results. This provided confidence that the EMS would perform well in different seasons and usage patterns before any live pilot.

* **Performance and Scalability:** Running one MILP per building per day is trivial for a modern server. If we had to manage 1000 homes, we could still solve each independently in parallel (since each is separate, unless we do community optimization). The agent-based design could be scaled by deploying multiple instances or threads. We did ensure the system can run in near-real-time for intraday updates if needed; for example, solving a 4-hour horizon MILP in a few seconds means one could even do it each hour. Our tests with up to 6 buildings combined in one MILP did show solve time increasing to tens of seconds, so for larger aggregations a hierarchical approach might be better (one MILP per building, then a top-level simpler optimization to coordinate, etc.). This is noted for future expansion.

In terms of deployment, our immediate plan is to deploy the EMS prototype in a **pilot building** in Curaçao (for instance, a test household or a section of a university campus). There, it would run with real hardware: smart plugs or direct control to AC units, etc., and initially just provide feedback to users (advisory mode) to see how they interact. Then move to automated control gradually for willing participants. The modular design and containerization should make this field deployment relatively straightforward, needing mainly to hook into the right data sources (the utility’s meter data, etc.). Additionally, because it’s containerized, if the local environment in Curaçao lacks certain compute resources, we could run the EMS in the cloud and just have a lightweight client on-site (though reliance on internet might be an issue; a hybrid approach is possible).

To conclude the methodology: we combined data-driven modeling and optimization in a cohesive pipeline, supported by modern software practices (tracking, containers, modular code). The choices made were aimed at ensuring the EMS is **not just an academic algorithm, but a deployable solution** that meets practical requirements like reliability, security, and user-friendliness. The next section will demonstrate how this methodology plays out in results and what benefits it concretely brings.

## Results and Discussion

After implementing the EMS, we conducted extensive simulations and a limited real-world pilot to evaluate its performance across multiple dimensions. This section presents a comprehensive analysis of the system's effectiveness in terms of key business and technical metrics: cost savings and economic benefits, peak load reduction and grid impact, PV self-consumption and renewable utilization, user comfort and acceptance, and computational efficiency. We also discuss strategic insights gained, such as the value proposition of battery storage integration, the effect of user preference weighting on adoption, and how the European-derived solution might generalize to the Caribbean context with its unique energy challenges.

The results demonstrate not only the technical feasibility of our approach but also its business viability and potential for market impact. By quantifying both the direct economic benefits to users and the broader system-level advantages, we provide a holistic view of the EMS's value proposition for different stakeholders in the energy ecosystem.

### Experimental Setup and Evaluation Framework

We designed a comprehensive evaluation framework to assess both the technical performance and business value of the EMS across diverse scenarios. Our test portfolio comprised **seven prototypical buildings** using a 90-day dataset spanning different seasons to ensure robust results across varying conditions. The building portfolio included:

* **Five residential scenarios** with varying occupancy patterns, solar capacity, and battery configurations, representing different household types from energy-conscious prosumers to standard consumers
* **One residential building without PV/battery** to establish baseline performance in traditional settings
* **One small industrial/commercial building** with consistent daytime load but flexibility in scheduling certain processes, representing the small business segment

For the Netherlands context, we used actual 2024 hourly dynamic prices from the Dutch day-ahead market and historical weather data for solar generation forecasting. For the Curaçao context simulation, since dynamic pricing isn't yet implemented, we developed a synthetic scenario where the current flat tariff was translated into an equivalent dynamic profile with realistic variations. This allowed us to evaluate potential benefits in anticipation of future tariff reforms while focusing on PV utilization and load shifting opportunities in the island context.

To comprehensively evaluate the EMS's performance and business value proposition, we compared four distinct operational scenarios:

1. **Baseline (Unoptimized)** – Devices operate according to typical usage patterns unaffected by price signals, representing the current state without intervention (our reference for cost and peak demand)
2. **Rule-Based Control** – A simple heuristic strategy where devices avoid the top 25% highest price hours but otherwise follow standard patterns, representing a low-complexity alternative
3. **EMS Cost-Optimized** – Our MILP optimization focusing purely on cost minimization without user preference weighting (essentially $w_{prob}=0$), showing the theoretical maximum savings potential
4. **EMS Balanced** – Our full system with preference weighting and all features activated, representing the realistic market-ready solution we propose

Additionally, we conducted specialized scenarios to isolate specific value drivers, such as **Full DER Integration** (EMS with PV+battery coordination) versus without battery storage, to quantify the incremental value of each system component. This approach allowed us to build a comprehensive business case with clear value attribution for different market segments and technology configurations.

### Cost Savings and Economic Benefits

Across all test buildings, the EMS achieved significant cost savings compared to the baseline. In the **no-battery scenario**, savings ranged from about **0.5% up to 36%** of the energy cost. The low end (0.5%) was the industrial building which had very inflexible load (it only shifted a small portion to slightly cheaper hours). The high end (\~36%) was a residential case with an EV and flexible appliances where there was big price variation and the EMS could shift almost everything (EV charging, water heating, washing) to cheap periods. A typical household saw on the order of 10–15% bill reduction with just smart scheduling (no battery). This aligns with literature expectations and is substantial given minimal inconvenience.

When **battery storage** is added, the savings can dramatically increase. Our results showed “With-battery” cost savings from **2% up to an astounding 450%** in one extreme case. How is >100% possible? Essentially, some homes became net *exporters* of energy (earning more from selling stored solar than they pay for grid usage). For example, one house with a large PV array and battery ended up making a net profit over the 90-day period, because the EMS stored excess solar and sold it during peak price hours, whereas in baseline that solar might have been curtailed or sold at low feed-in rates. Those percentage figures (200–450%) reflect that baseline cost was low or near zero (due to PV covering a lot), and EMS turned it into a net gain or zero cost, so the relative improvement is huge. In absolute terms, some residences saved **€26–€111 per day** once batteries were in play, which is very high but those days are ones where baseline cost was high (e.g. an EV charging on grid vs now EV charging from solar and even selling some battery power at peak). The industrial site saw only modest battery benefits, because it didn’t have PV and battery was used just for peak shaving; still it got an extra few percent savings.

These results confirm that **battery integration is key** for maximizing value. It not only lets a home avoid buying expensive grid power (by using stored cheap power), but if allowed, it can arbitrage by selling at high price periods. Our model did include degradation cost to ensure this is economically sound (we wouldn’t cycle a battery just to make €0.01 arbitrage and wear it out). The big numbers came when peak prices were \~€0.70/kWh and midday were €0.10 – a battery could earn a lot in that spread even with degradation.

**User Preference Impact:** When we compare the cost-only optimization to the balanced (cost+comfort), we found that the balanced EMS gave up a small fraction of the potential savings in order to keep users happy. For example, in one case cost-only EMS saved 15% but at the expense of running laundry at 3am (user unhappy); the balanced EMS saved \~13% but kept laundry at 6am (within user preferred window). So the “cost” of respecting preferences was maybe 1–3% of the bill (which we deem acceptable). Meanwhile, **user satisfaction** metrics (measured as % of appliance operations within preferred times) stayed above 85–90% as targeted. We even allowed users to weight their own discomfort cost higher, and as expected, the EMS would then practically stick 100% to normal usage times with only minor scheduling tweaks, yielding perhaps only 5% savings – a trade-off each deployment can configure.

**Energy Poverty and Bills:** One discussion point is how this translates to annual savings and impact. For a Dutch household with an annual bill of e.g. €1200, a 15% EMS saving is \~€180/year, which is meaningful. For a Curaçao household, currently with no dynamic pricing, immediate savings come from PV/battery usage optimization. If dynamic pricing or even TOU (day vs night rate) is introduced, similar percentages could apply. Considering that in Curaçao energy is \~\$0.26/kWh and an average household might use \~500 kWh/month (just an estimate), that’s \$130/month. A 10% saving is \$13/month – modest but in a year \~\$156, which in a lower-income context is valuable (and could cover other necessities). The EMS also has non-monetary benefits like informing users of their consumption which can lead to behavioral savings (some studies show just giving feedback yields 5-10% conservation).

### Peak Demand and Grid Impact

One key goal was to reduce peak demand on the grid. We measured the highest hourly grid import for each building with and without EMS. In residences, the baseline often had an evening peak (everyone cooks, EV plugged in, etc., simultaneously). The EMS schedules things sequentially rather than concurrently if possible (for example, it won’t have the water heater and EV charge at the exact same peak hour if a slight delay still achieves goals). We observed **peak demand reductions of 20–30% per building** on average in cost-only optimization. In the balanced mode, it was slightly less, maybe \~15%, because sometimes user preferences force some concurrency. Still, this is significant: e.g., a house that used to draw 5 kW peak now draws only 3.5–4 kW at peak after EMS. If scaled to many houses, this could lower the distribution transformer loads notably.

We also tested a “Peak Coordination” scenario where multiple buildings are optimized together to specifically reduce the **coincident peak** of the group. The EMS was able to stagger some loads between houses (like not all water heaters at 6pm, some at 6, some at 7, etc.). The result was an even greater community-level peak shaving (\~8% additional reduction beyond individual EMS). This suggests future virtual power plant or community EMS approaches could leverage house-to-house coordination for grid services.

For the grid in Curaçao, even without dynamic pricing, if the utility gave a signal like “please reduce load in this hour”, an EMS could respond uniformly across homes to achieve that. We consider such demand response signals as future integration; our simulation of a “coordinated optimization” hints at its feasibility.

Also noteworthy is **load profile smoothing**: we plotted before/after load curves. Typically, the EMS curve is flatter, with some shifts to late night or midday (if solar). This not only helps peak but can improve **load factor** (ratio of average to peak load). Better load factor means the grid is used more evenly, improving efficiency.

### User Comfort and Acceptance

User comfort was a paramount consideration, and we evaluated it both quantitatively and qualitatively. Quantitatively, we tracked the percentage of appliance operations that occurred within user-preferred times. In our balanced EMS runs, this was typically 90–100%. A slight drop below 100% happened if the user’s requested window was very tight and prices extremely favorable outside it – the EMS might nudge something slightly out (with permission). For example, if a user said “finish dishwashing by 9pm” and normally they run it at 7pm, but one day 9pm is dramatically cheaper, the EMS might ask, “Can we run it finishing at 9:30pm for more savings?” If the user had allowed some flexibility, it might do it (but if not, it strictly wouldn’t). These scenarios were rare in simulation; mostly the EMS found solutions within constraints.

We also simulated some **override scenarios**: if a user spontaneously starts an appliance at a non-scheduled time, the EMS detects it (through the device agent) and simply updates the plan – e.g., cancels the later run it had planned. This way, the user never loses control. The cost is maybe slightly higher that day but comfort is maintained. We measured that such overrides (if happening, say, 1 in 10 times) hardly dent the overall savings because the EMS just adapts. This is important for acceptance: users need to know they can always do what they want, and the system will adapt around them, not vice versa.

In surveys or feedback (from a small pilot group who saw the concept via the UI demo), users were receptive when they saw the potential savings and the fact that they could always override. Some expressed concerns about specific appliances (e.g., “I don’t want my laundry done at night because of noise” – which is precisely why we have preference settings). Once they saw they could set that, they were fine. Education is key; an EMS should probably start in a recommendation/feedback mode (“we suggest you run dryer at 2pm to save \$X”) and only after trust is built, move to automatic control with permission. We foresee this transition in any real deployment.

The EMS also maintained comfort in terms of not violating any constraints: for example, hot water heaters and HVAC – we set those such that the temperature settings weren’t compromised, just timing of heating/cooling was shifted a bit. No user would notice a difference in temperature, only in bill. In EV charging, the car was always ready by the set departure time in our simulation (we tested random earlier departures to see if EMS could occasionally fail, but we had built enough buffer that it did not fail in tests).

### Computational and Operational Performance

The EMS ran efficiently: an average solve time of \~8.2 seconds per day means it’s trivial to do on a regular PC. Memory usage was also small (the MILP had on the order of a few thousand variables and constraints). The machine learning model updates took a fraction of a second each day (since we’re just updating counts or running a small prediction). This shows the approach is computationally feasible even on embedded hardware. If implementing on a smart home hub, it could work (especially if using CPLEX or Gurobi, those times could be cut further, or by simplifying MILP if needed).

From an operational standpoint, one potential issue is reliability of data feeds – if price data or weather forecast is delayed, the EMS schedule could be suboptimal. We addressed this by: if new prices aren’t in by a certain time, use a fallback (like yesterday’s prices or a default TOU) so at least some schedule is produced. This is important in Curaçao context where internet might drop; the EMS should have a reasonable behavior offline (maybe stick to last known good schedule or a fixed time-of-use plan).

### Generalization to Curaçao and Other Regions

One of our core questions was: how well does a solution developed with European data apply to the Caribbean context? Based on simulations and qualitative analysis:

* **Dynamic Pricing Readiness:** The EMS is ready to leverage dynamic pricing whenever it’s introduced. In Curaçao, even a simpler TOU (say day vs night rate) would yield some savings (the EMS would concentrate usage at night if cheaper, etc.). Our simulation of a hypothetical peak/off-peak tariff (with off-peak at night 30% cheaper) showed \~8% savings just from that, mostly by EV charging and water heating at night. That’s purely due to pricing, not PV (which remains same). So even modest tariff reforms could be aided by EMS to maximize consumer benefit.

* **Solar and Storage:** Curaçao has abundant sun; solar is an attractive investment there. Our EMS would be extremely useful for anyone with PV, because maximizing self-consumption when feed-in might not be rewarded is key. If the utility at some point restricts net metering (common as solar penetration grows), EMS becomes almost necessary to use your solar effectively. So, the European context of rising PV is similar. One difference: in Curaçao, air conditioning is a major flexible load (whereas in NL, space heating and EV are major ones). We already handle HVAC as a flexible load in concept (we did simulate a thermostat that pre-cools or delays cooling a bit). We might need to tweak models for AC demand patterns (possibly use cooling degree hours instead of occupancy for probability of AC usage). But the framework supports it.

* **Grid Constraints:** The Curaçao grid might have stricter limits on export or even on max demand per house. Our EMS can incorporate those easily (just set \$G^{max}\$ in grid constraints). If anything, the need to avoid peaks is more pronounced on a smaller grid, so EMS’s peak shave feature is valuable. During our engagement with local stakeholders, another use-case emerged: **generator optimization**. Some remote areas or backup systems rely on diesel generators. An EMS can schedule loads to minimize generator fuel use (e.g., run certain loads when solar is on so generator throttles down or fuel is saved). This wasn’t explicitly simulated, but it’s analogous to dynamic pricing (just that the “price” is high when on generator). The logic would hold.

* **Cultural adoption:** One possible challenge is user adoption in a different culture – technology acceptance might vary. However, given mobile phone penetration is high even in developing regions, an app-based interface to control energy might be welcomed if it clearly saves money. The concept of energy budgeting is very relevant in Curaçao, where incomes are lower on average and electricity is a significant expense. If the EMS can show “you saved X dollars this month” and help them plan (maybe not directly because price flat, but by reducing waste), it has a compelling narrative.

* **Data availability:** One concern: our ML models learn from usage data. If a household in Curaçao has no historical data or smart meter, the system would start cold. We addressed this by using generalized probability profiles (from other similar households or from our Dutch sample adjusted for local behavior). Essentially, EMS can start with a default schedule (e.g., assume people use appliances in evening) and then adapt once it observes a few days/weeks of real usage. We also incorporate the idea of user input to seed preferences (“when do you usually run your washing machine?” – if user provides that, it sets initial probabilities accordingly). So even in a data-sparse environment, the system can be bootstrapped.

In conclusion, the results demonstrate that the EMS achieved its primary objectives. We saw **double-digit percentage cost savings** in most cases, **improved renewable usage**, and **peak load reductions**, all while keeping users largely satisfied and requiring minimal computational resources. The approach appears **scalable and adaptable** beyond the test conditions. There is always uncertainty in how lab simulations translate to the real world, but the consistency of results with expectations (and with other research) gives confidence. In a real pilot in Curaçao, the key metrics to watch will be user interaction and actual savings (especially since pricing structure is different). We expect slightly lower percentage savings there initially (due to flat pricing, you only save by using more solar or cutting waste), but the qualitative benefits of awareness and readiness for future tariffs are significant.

### Strategic Value Proposition

The integration of probabilistic user behavior modeling directly into optimization frameworks offers a novel approach to addressing uncertainty in demand response, complementing existing stochastic or robust optimization methods. Our work demonstrates how "soft" constraints via penalty costs can effectively align automated systems with human preferences - an increasingly important consideration as AI systems become more deeply integrated with human-driven systems. The performance metrics we've documented (e.g., MILP solve times of seconds, predictive accuracy AUC ~0.8-0.9) provide valuable benchmarks for future researchers exploring integrated AI and operations research solutions in the energy domain.

From an industry perspective, the EMS represents a market-ready solution addressing multiple pressing challenges:

**For Utilities and Grid Operators:**
* **Infrastructure investment deferral** through peak demand reduction
* **Improved grid stability** and power quality through smoother aggregate demand profiles
* **Enhanced renewable integration capacity** without costly grid reinforcement
* **New business models** around flexibility services and virtual power plants

**For Energy Service Companies:**
* **Differentiated service offerings** with demonstrable customer value
* **Recurring revenue opportunities** through subscription or performance-based models
* **Platform for additional energy services** including maintenance, auditing, and equipment upgrades
* **Data-driven customer insights** enabling targeted cross-selling opportunities

**For End Users:**
* **Immediate cost savings** with minimal lifestyle adjustments
* **Enhanced energy awareness** leading to additional behavioral conservation
* **Maximized value** from investments in solar PV and battery storage
* **Future-proofing** against evolving energy market structures and pricing models

**For Society and Policy Objectives:**
* **Accelerated renewable adoption** through improved economics and integration
* **Reduced carbon emissions** through optimization and waste reduction
* **Energy poverty alleviation** through cost management and education
* **Energy resilience** at both individual building and community levels

In regions like Curaçao, the EMS could become a cornerstone for addressing energy affordability challenges. By coupling the technology with educational programs, households could actively manage and reduce their energy expenses with minimal lifestyle changes. The system's adaptability to different pricing structures means it can deliver immediate value through solar optimization while positioning users to benefit from future market reforms.

Moreover, the EMS framework is inherently scalable from individual buildings to community or island-wide applications. The same principles that optimize a single household can be extended to manage microgrids or clusters of buildings, contributing significantly to grid resilience through coordinated response to supply constraints or contingency events.

### Future Development Roadmap

Building on this project's success, we have identified several high-potential avenues for further development:

**Near-Term Priorities (0-12 months):**

* **Pilot Deployment Program:** Implement field pilots in diverse building types across both target regions. These deployments will provide invaluable feedback on user interaction patterns, system reliability under real-world conditions, and actual savings verification. They will also surface any integration challenges with physical devices that weren't fully captured in simulation environments.

* **User Interface Refinement:** Based on pilot feedback, enhance the user experience with more intuitive visualizations, simplified preference setting mechanisms, and clearer communication of system decisions and benefits. Develop region-specific interfaces accounting for cultural and market differences between European and Caribbean contexts.

* **Hardware Integration Expansion:** Extend device compatibility to include additional smart appliance brands, HVAC systems, and renewable energy equipment. Develop standardized integration protocols to simplify onboarding of new device types.

**Medium-Term Objectives (1-2 years):**

* **Enhanced Forecasting Capabilities:** Incorporate more sophisticated forecasting techniques for both load prediction and renewable generation. Potential approaches include neural network models for short-term load forecasting and integration of real-time weather data for improved PV production estimates. These enhancements could further improve optimization outcomes by 5-10%.

* **Multi-objective Optimization Framework:** Evolve beyond the current weighted-sum approach to explicitly handle multi-objective optimization with Pareto-optimal trade-offs between competing goals (cost, comfort, carbon footprint). Develop interfaces allowing users to dynamically adjust their preferences between "green" and "economical" operation modes.

* **Community-Scale Coordination:** Extend the system architecture to support neighborhood-level optimization, enabling coordinated operation across multiple buildings. This would unlock additional value through shared resources, improved aggregate load profiles, and collective participation in grid service markets.

**Long-Term Vision (2+ years):**

* **Grid Service Integration:** Develop capabilities for participation in formal grid service markets, including demand response programs, frequency regulation, and capacity markets. This would create new revenue streams for users while supporting broader grid stability objectives.

* **Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) Integration:** Extend the EMS to support bidirectional EV charging, allowing electric vehicles to serve as mobile energy resources. Our architecture could accommodate a dedicated V2G Agent that manages this functionality under appropriate economic and user preference conditions.

* **Peer-to-Peer Energy Trading:** Position the EMS as an enabler for local energy markets, where prosumers can directly trade excess generation with neighbors. This would require additional market logic and settlement mechanisms but could build on the existing optimization framework.

* **AI-Driven Continuous Improvement:** Implement more advanced machine learning techniques for ongoing system optimization, including reinforcement learning approaches that continuously refine control strategies based on observed outcomes and changing conditions.

This development roadmap ensures that the EMS remains at the forefront of energy management technology while delivering increasing value to all stakeholders. By maintaining our dual focus on technical excellence and practical business applications, we can ensure that this solution makes a meaningful contribution to the global energy transition while creating sustainable commercial opportunities.

### Future Work

* **Economic Modeling:** Future research could integrate dynamic economic optimization over longer horizons, such as considering battery degradation and replacement in a lifecycle cost manner, or suggesting to the user when an investment (like adding a battery or more PV) would pay off given their usage profile and the EMS optimization. Essentially, the EMS could evolve into an energy advisor, not just an operational optimizer.
* **Policy and Scalability Studies:** On a larger scale, working with policymakers to simulate what happens if, say, 50% of homes adopt EMS. How much peak reduction on the national grid? How much CO2 reduction if powered by more solar? These studies can support business cases and perhaps incentives for adoption (e.g., government rebates for smart EMS installations, similar to solar rebates).
* **AI and Reinforcement Learning:** An exploratory direction is using reinforcement learning (RL) to have the EMS learn optimal strategies over time, possibly in scenarios where the environment is complex. RL could potentially handle non-linear dynamics or complex comfort metrics better. However, MILP’s transparency and guarantees are a strength, so RL would likely complement, not replace, our MILP (perhaps providing good heuristic solutions or coordinating multiple buildings in a non-linear way).
* **User Experience:** Continue improving the user interface and experience. Gamification elements could be added (e.g., showing a user’s energy savings achievements, comparing with neighbors in a friendly competition) to increase engagement. Also, more granular control for users who want it (some might want to tweak more, others will want it fully automatic). This also involves further human factors research: understanding how much automation people are comfortable with, and how to present recommendations in a persuasive yet transparent way.
